# Cars vs LLMs: You wouldn’t choose a car on fuel price alone.

So why judge AI by the price of its tokens?

The real comparison includes what you need to do, how reliably you get there, and how much driving you have to do yourself.

<CarsIllustration eager />

LLMs are the language models behind many AI assistants. Tokens are the small pieces of text they process: sometimes whole words, sometimes parts of words. Many providers charge for the amount of text going in and coming out. That makes token prices useful for comparing certain running costs.

If you use a monthly subscription, you may never look at token prices. You might compare subscription fees, response speed or model rankings instead. Each tells you something. None, on its own, tells you whether the AI suits your work.

Knowing what petrol costs does not tell you whether you need a hatchback or a van. It does not even tell you how much petrol either will use.

We already understand this when choosing cars. We ask what we’ll use them for, what they can carry, how they behave and whether we trust them to get us home. Nobody responds to “Which car should I buy?” with “Whichever uses the cheapest petrol” and expects to remain in charge of the conversation.

We can bring the same common sense to choosing an AI.

## Start with the job

Suppose you’re planning a family weekend in Paphos. You need somewhere to stay, a manageable itinerary and enough flexibility that the holiday survives contact with your children. You have a budget, a few preferences and two days.

Which AI should help?

That depends on what “help” means.

If you already have everything booked and want your notes turned into a tidy itinerary, the job is fairly straightforward. If you need someone to compare accommodation, check travel times, reconcile conflicting information and fit everything around a toddler’s nap, you’re asking considerably more.

Both requests might begin with “Plan my weekend.” They are different jobs.

Choosing an AI starts with the same question as choosing a car: what are you going to use it for?

A city car, a family hatchback, a work van and an off-roader earn their place by doing different jobs well. AI models can be approached in much the same way.

These are loose categories. A model can cover several roles, and the tools and settings you use can change what it is good at. The point is to start with the work rather than assume that the most powerful option must be the right one.

For our Paphos weekend, tidying confirmed bookings into an itinerary is one job. Comparing accommodation, checking current information and working around several constraints is another. The second needs more than a talent for making a packing list look presentable.

## Read the spec sheet

Once you know what kind of help you need, compare the qualities that will actually affect the result.

Fuel price matters. So do consumption, reliability, capacity and handling. And then there is the driver: you.

The comparison is deliberately imperfect. More room for documents does not guarantee that their details will be understood, and an answer delivered quickly may still take considerable work to become useful.

Use the table to decide what deserves a test drive. For the weekend plan, current information and reliable handling of your constraints may matter most. For a quick rewrite, speed and following your preferred tone may be enough.

The same approach applies to comparing supplier proposals, fixing a spreadsheet or drafting a customer email.

One good answer cannot establish consistency. Try a few tasks typical of the work you need, give each candidate comparable briefs and requirements, and notice whether the same problems recur.

For our weekend, the itinerary might look beautiful while quietly forgetting the budget, putting lunch after the restaurant closes or inventing a hotel amenity.

The prose can remain immaculate throughout. Apparently, being wrong is no obstacle to excellent presentation.

This is a limit of the car analogy worth recognising as we go. An AI can produce a plausible answer without establishing that it is true. Confidence of tone is not a dashboard warning light. Important details still need checking.

And not every mistake comes from the model. If you forget to mention that you’re travelling with children, it cannot reliably plan around them. But a clear brief is not a magic spell either. You can provide the right information and still receive the wrong answer.

## A quick answer isn’t always a quick result

There is the speed at which an answer appears, and the time it takes to get something usable.

An itinerary delivered in five seconds feels fast. If you then spend twenty minutes removing impossible stops, restoring the nap and asking why dinner is forty kilometres from the hotel, the overall experience is less impressive.

An answer that takes longer but needs fewer repairs may get you finished sooner. Equally, a slow answer can still be wrong. Waiting is not evidence of wisdom.

<CarsIllustration kind="repair-time" />

Lower token prices alone cannot settle efficiency: one attempt may involve more text, more tool use or more retries than another. For now, the useful question is whether the result justifies the resources and effort required.

Then there is capacity.

Your weekend plan might begin with three sentences. Later, you could have accommodation descriptions, booking confirmations, restaurant menus and a long message from your partner explaining what “relaxing” is supposed to mean.

That material needs to fit, but fitting it all into an AI request does not guarantee that every detail will be noticed or used correctly. A large boot holds your suitcase. It does not promise to understand your packing decisions.

Tools and features depend partly on the product you’re using, not just the model. An excellent engine does not, by itself, provide navigation.

A longer feature list is useful only if something on it helps you.

There are also choices about how the vehicle is operated. Running a model yourself is closer to keeping your own vehicle and garage: you gain control over the setup and take on its maintenance. An AI agent is closer to having a driver: it can carry out steps on your behalf, within the tools and permissions you give it.

Neither arrangement tells you how capable the underlying model is. You still need to know whether it can do the job—and when you need to intervene.

Which brings us to the person using it.

You choose the destination, explain the constraints and decide what to trust. Some assistants can carry out more steps themselves; others need frequent direction. Either way, the amount of supervision required is part of their suitability.

You should not need a qualification in asking questions to plan a weekend. But saying what matters—budget, children, pace, travel dates—gives the system a fairer chance than “Make it amazing.”

## Take it for a test drive

The practical way to choose is to try the AI on work you recognise. Use the same information and standard to compare candidates, and look beyond the answer that initially sounds most impressive.

Ask:

- Can it do the job I actually need, with the information and tools involved?
- Does it get the important things right consistently?
- How much checking, correcting and steering does it require from me?

Cars and language models behave differently, but those questions travel well.

Ultimately, people do not buy engines. They are trying to get somewhere.

Choosing a suitable AI is the first step. The next question is what the whole journey costs: the money, time and effort it takes to turn a promising answer into completed work.
