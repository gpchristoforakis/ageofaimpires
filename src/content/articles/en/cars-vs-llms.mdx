# You Wouldn’t Choose a Car by Petrol Price Alone

**Cheap fuel means nothing if the car keeps taking you the wrong way.**

The same goes for AI.

The cheapest option is rarely the cheapest way to get something done. Neither is the fastest. And the one that produces the most impressive first reply may still leave you doing half the driving yourself.

Because the destination is not the first answer.

**The destination is finished work.** Everything before that is just the journey.

## 1. Where am I going?

Start with the job. Not the AI. Not the subscription fee. Not the shiny list of things it claims to do.

**The job.**

Suppose you’re planning a family weekend in Paphos. You need somewhere to stay, a manageable itinerary, a few good places to eat, and enough flexibility for the holiday to survive contact with your children.

You have two days, a strict budget, and a strong desire not to spend Saturday driving backwards and forwards across the island.

You ask an AI to plan the weekend. But what does “plan the weekend” actually mean?

Maybe everything is already booked. You simply want your reservations, notes, and places to visit organised into a tidy schedule. Easy enough.

Or maybe nothing is booked.

Now the assistant has to compare places to stay, respect your budget, check real driving times, fit lunch around a toddler’s nap, and avoid putting dinner forty kilometres away from the hotel.

Both jobs begin with the exact same request: *“Plan my weekend.”*

They are not the same journey.

Choosing an AI without first defining the job is like walking into a dealership and asking: *“Which car should I buy?”*

The only sensible reply is: *“Where are you going?”*

## 2. Is this the right car?

Nobody buys a car simply because it is “the best car”. Best for what?

A compact city car is perfect for squeezing through tight streets and hopeless for moving house. A van will swallow all your furniture but feels ridiculous on a quick school run. An off-roader only earns its keep when the tarmac ends.

The job decides what makes sense. AI deserves the exact same test.

If you just need a short email tightened up, you don’t need an expedition vehicle. But if you’re juggling ten options, dates, budgets, and several things that all have to work at once, the little city runabout may start to struggle.

More is not automatically better. Neither is bigger, faster, or more expensive. **More horsepower doesn’t help when what you actually needed was a van.**

## 3. How much fuel will it burn?

This is where headline prices get misleading.

Petrol at €1 a litre sounds great until you realise the car burns twice as much of it getting to the destination.

Imagine two assistants planning that Paphos weekend.

**The first gives you an itinerary almost instantly.** It looks immaculate. Hotel. Beach. Lunch. Afternoon stop. Dinner.

**Done.**

Except lunch starts after the kitchen closes. The beach is on the wrong side of the island. Dinner is forty kilometres away. And somewhere along the road, the toddler’s afternoon nap was abolished by executive order.

So you go back. Fix lunch. Move dinner. Restore the nap. Find another beach. Check the hotel. Ask again. Correct again.

What looked like a fast, cheap trip turned into three laps around the roundabout.

The second assistant takes a minute longer to answer. But the route makes sense. The opening times work. The budget holds.

Which one was faster?

The stopwatch says the first. Your Saturday evening says the second.

**A quick answer is not a quick result.** The clock only stops when the work is usable. And the real cost of the journey isn’t just the fuel you bought at the start. It’s the fuel you burned on detours. Plus your time. Plus the hassle.

## 4. How many wrong turns will I take?

Speed is seductive. Getting an answer five seconds after asking feels like magic.

But five seconds to somewhere is not five seconds to your destination. If the next twenty minutes are spent repairing mistakes, that five-second miracle looks a lot less impressive.

The reverse is equally true. A slow answer can still be wrong. **Waiting is not evidence of wisdom.**

And one successful attempt doesn’t tell you much either. **One good trip proves nothing.**

An assistant can invent a hotel amenity, blow past your budget, or recommend a closed restaurant - all while sounding completely composed and authoritative.

**Confidence {"doesn't"} come with a dashboard warning light.**

If something matters, check it. Then try another journey. Does the car keep getting you there every time you turn the key? Or does every trip bring a new breakdown?

Reliability isn’t pulling off one brilliant party trick. It’s delivering finished work repeatedly without turning you into an unpaid roadside mechanic.

## 5. How much driving am I doing myself?

This is the cost people forget: **You.**

Your time. Your attention. Your corrections. Your patience while explaining for the third time that dinner cannot happen before lunch.

**Every minute spent steering an assistant back onto the road belongs on the invoice.**

Some steering is reasonable. If you forget to mention you are travelling with two small children, don’t be surprised when the itinerary suggests a midnight bar crawl. The driver still sets the destination and supplies the rules.

But there is a massive difference between setting the destination and having to grab the wheel every thirty seconds to avoid hitting a lamppost.

The same applies to luggage.

Your weekend plan might start with three sentences. Soon, you add hotel confirmations, restaurant menus, places you want to visit, and a long message from your partner explaining what the word “relaxing” actually means.

Now there’s luggage. **A bigger boot holds your suitcase. It does not promise to understand your packing decisions.** Giving an assistant all the details doesn’t guarantee it will notice or use them properly.

And finally, check the dashboard. Heated seats are lovely in Helsinki. They are rather less persuasive in August in Paphos. **A button you never need {"isn't"} a feature. {"It's"} dashboard clutter.**

What matters is whether the car has what this journey actually requires.

## Take it for a test drive

Most people judge AI by what happens at the start of the trip. How fast did it respond? How much did it cost? How confident did it sound?

It all looks rather less impressive if you’re still stranded halfway there.

So don’t compare promises. **Give them the same job:**

- Same details.
- Same rules.
- Same destination.

Then look at what actually happened. Did it finish the work? Did the important details survive the journey? How many wrong turns did it take? How much fixing did you do? How much checking? How much steering?

How long before you could genuinely say: *Done.*

People don’t buy cars to admire engines. They buy them to get somewhere.

AI is no different.

**The first answer is not the destination. Finished work is. So stop measuring how fast the first answer appears. Start asking what it actually cost you to arrive.**
