PART 01

The Price You See (BUT PROBABLY DON’T UNDERSTAND)

For many AI APIs, the most visible price is the token rate. Major providers publish input and output prices per million tokens.

This pricing setup creates a persistent optical illusion. It encourages buyers to evaluate technology options the same way they purchase bulk electricity or office paper—by looking strictly at the unit price on the bill. Under this view, a system charging pennies per attempt can look more economical than one charging higher rates for the same run.

The flaw in this logic is simple: raw computation is not a finished product.

You are not buying raw computer activity. You are buying a finished, verified result—a customer support query answered correctly, an invoice processed into a database, or a messy document converted into a clean table.

The advertised rate card tells you only what it costs to start the engine. It tells you nothing about how many times that engine might stall, how much extra road it may cover without asking, or how often a human worker will have to step in and drive. When a system fails a basic formatting rule repeatedly, or when it wanders off into an unasked-for entry, the advertised unit price loses its meaning. The real cost is determined by what happens after the prompt is sent.

PART 02

The Cost You Actually Pay

To see the true price of an automated job, it helps to use a practical mental framework: Effective Task Cost.

Effective Task Cost tracks every expense required to move a job from an initial request to an acceptable finish line. It breaks the real cost down into four parts:

Effective Task Cost
direct AI cost
+ retries and corrections
+ unnecessary extra work
+ human intervention
  • Direct AI cost: The baseline fee charged for the initial attempt. This is the visible sticker price based on input and output usage.
  • Retries and corrections: The cost of running the system again when the first answer fails a formatting rule or delivers an incomplete result.
  • Unnecessary extra work: The cost of extra processing consumed when a system writes rambling explanations, explores side questions, or over-complicates a simple request.
  • Human intervention: The price of employee time spent inspecting output, fixing errors, steering stuck workflows, or completing unfinished steps manually.

When you look at automated tasks through this lens, cheap computation can turn out to be a trap. Cutting direct software charges down to fractions of a cent backfires if it forces employees to spend time fixing many outputs. Completed work, not raw usage, is the economic unit that matters.

PART 03

When Cheap Creates More Repair Work

A common way an automated setup becomes expensive is through repeated repair work.

When a setup does not enforce structured output — or when a structurally valid answer still fails the business rules — the surrounding system may be unable to use the result.

For example, without enforced structured output, a model might add conversational text like “Here is the data you requested!” above a structured entry, causing a pipeline expecting clean data to break.

When a failure occurs, the workflow has to decide what happens next. It may run an automatic retry, sending the prompt again with instructions to fix the problem. That adds compute cost. If retries still fail, the task may land on an employee's desk.

This is where the economics break down. Cheap computation can create expensive human attention. Saving a fraction of a cent on the initial request is counterproductive if it causes human labour expense while an employee opens the file, spots the missing field, corrects the text, and submits the record manually.

PART 04

When Powerful AI Does Too Much

Some AI setups fail in the opposite direction: the model does more than the task requires.

More Intelligence Does Not Automatically Mean More Discipline.

Consider a simple daily task: taking a transcript of a team meeting and pulling out a short list of assigned action items.

A capable system given an open-ended prompt may not stop at three bullet points. Depending on its system instructions and setup, it may write an introductory summary, analyse team sentiment, outline potential project risks, and draft follow-up emails for every participant.

Where providers charge based on output volume, this unprompted extra work directly inflates the bill. The system generates extra text that nobody asked for, nobody needed, and nobody will read.

When a system spends time and money solving problems you never gave it, its capability becomes a source of financial leakage. High capability without clear boundaries is just expensive drift.

PART 05

Why Multi-Step AI Compounds Waste

The gap between advertised prices and real costs widens when systems handle multi-step workflows.

In a single-step task—like summarising an entry—an error or an extra paragraph is an isolated nuisance. But multi-step systems often operate as chains: Step 1 extracts key details from a document, Step 2 calculates a financial total, and Step 3 drafts a confirmation note.

In a chain, inefficiencies compound.

Hypothetical Example
0.90 × 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.90 ≈ 0.656

As a hypothetical illustration, assuming each step in a four-step process operates independently with a 90% chance of succeeding cleanly on its first try, the probability of the entire chain completing cleanly on the first pass drops below 66%.

In the remaining cases, early errors can be passed forward. A minor formatting flaw in Step 1 may force Step 2 to run retry passes. If retry output is carried into later steps, the next stage may have more material to process. By the time the task reaches the final step, the system may be spending additional time and compute dealing with earlier mistakes. Multi-step automation can turn small inefficiencies into larger ones.

PART 06

The Completion Boundary

Why do automated systems struggle to know when to stop?

A common reason is the gap between a technical stopping rule and the human definition of a finished job.

Software can stop because it reaches a time, usage or step limit. That does not mean the human job is finished.

A technical stop is not the same as a finished job. Systems need clear completion criteria to reliably match the user's definition of “done.” A job is finished only when real-world requirements are met:

  • All required fields in the record are accurately filled.
  • The output passes a database validation check.
  • The calculated total matches the purchase order.
  • The finalised file is saved in the right place.

A system can hit a technical stop while leaving the job completely unfinished—halting halfway through a data file and leaving it broken. Conversely, a system might satisfy the business requirement in its first three lines, but keep processing for another ten paragraphs because no one set a boundary.

Without explicit criteria to match the user's definition of “done,” a system can alternate between stopping too early and working too long.

PART 07

One Simple Hypothetical Comparison

Consider a simplified hypothetical comparison involving a batch of customer intake records processed under two different setups.

Setup A — Lowest Unit Price

Charges a low rate per attempt. A noticeable portion of records fail the initial automated check. Automatic retries fix some, while remaining records require human correction.

Setup B — Higher Unit Price

Charges more per initial attempt, but follows formatting rules reliably. Nearly all records complete on the first pass and remaining edge cases resolve automatically.

Under Setup A, the direct software bill is low, but fixing the broken records requires hours of employee labour, adding significant human payroll expense.

Under Setup B, the software bill is higher upfront, but human labour costs are zero.

In this simplified example, even though Setup A looked far cheaper on the rate card, Setup B delivers a lower total cost per completed job. The apparent savings of Setup A disappear once employees have to step in.

PART 08

What To Measure Instead

If advertised token rates give a misleading picture of expenses, what should teams using AI track instead?

Rather than focusing on unit price cards, track three practical indicators:

  1. First-time success rate: The percentage of jobs that satisfy all rules on the very first try, without needing retries or error fixes.
  2. Human involvement: How often an employee has to step in, inspect, or correct an output before it can be used.
  3. Cost per completed job: Total money spent—including direct software fees, automated retries, and employee labour time—divided by the number of verified, usable results.

Before deploying an automated process, put two practical guardrails in place:

  • Automatic validation checks: Wrap the output in an automated check—such as a database format test—that verifies whether the work meets acceptance rules before accepting it.
  • Task-specific retry ceilings: Set a firm limit on retries and spend per task appropriate to the workload. If a job fails its allocated retry limit, force the system to stop immediately and route it for review rather than letting it run up charges in a loop.
PART 09

Conclusion: Define The Finish Line Before Optimising The Price

The central mistake in evaluating artificial intelligence is confusing the price of computation with the cost of work.

Unit rates and token tables provide a clean, easily comparable metric, but they measure engine activity rather than finished business results. A cheap system that frequently fails leaves behind a trail of retries and employee repair work. A capable system left without strict boundaries can burn budget doing work no one requested.

Before comparing provider rate cards or attempting to optimise software spend, establish a clear finish line. Define what an acceptable result looks like, put automated checks in place to verify it, and set appropriate ceilings on how many attempts the system can make.

Measure The Cost Of Completed Work, Not Just The Cost Of Running The AI.
